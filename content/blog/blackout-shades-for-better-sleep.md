If you bought blackout shades and the room still glows at the edges, nothing is wrong with the fabric. Blackout fabric blocks the light that hits it. The light that wakes you up comes around it: the gaps at the sides, the halo at the headrail, the bright line under the bottom bar. Making a room truly dark is a fit and hardware problem, and it is a solvable one. This guide covers where the leaks come from, the three levels of fix, and who actually needs the full treatment.

## Why darkness is a sleep issue, not a comfort preference

Your body runs on a circadian rhythm, an internal clock that is set largely by light. When your eyes take in light, your brain reads it as daytime and slows or stops production of melatonin, the hormone that makes you drowsy and keeps you asleep. The [Sleep Foundation's overview of light and sleep](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/light-and-sleep) lays out the mechanism: darkness starts melatonin production, light exposure suppresses it, and a misaligned rhythm affects far more than how rested you feel.

The sources are ordinary. A streetlight across the road. Headlights sweeping the wall at 5 AM. A neighbor's porch light. Summer sunrises that arrive an hour before your alarm. None of them feel bright at noon. At 3 AM, to a brain deciding whether to stay asleep, they are a signal. That is why the goal is not a dimmer room. It is a dark one.

## Why most blackout shades still leak light

An inside-mounted shade needs clearance to travel up and down without rubbing the frame. That clearance is the light gap, and it is built into every standard installation. The fabric can be fully opaque and the room will still show three leaks:

- **The sides.** A bright outline on each edge of the shade, worst on east and west windows.
- **The top.** Light spills over the headrail or through the cassette gap.
- **The bottom.** The bottom bar stops at the sill, and any unevenness in the sill leaves a line of light.

This is also why the industry term is usually "room darkening" rather than blackout. Room darkening describes what the fabric does. It says nothing about the edges, and the edges are where sleep goes to die.

## The three levels of dark

**Level one: blackout fabric, well fitted.** A blackout cellular shade or blackout roller shade, measured tight and mounted correctly, kills direct light and leaves a soft edge glow. For most bedrooms this is enough, and it is where most people should start. [Cellular shades](/products/cellular-shades) add insulation value on top of the darkness; [roller shades](/products/roller-shades) give you the cleanest look and the widest blackout fabric selection.

**Level two: sealed systems.** This is where true darkness lives. Side channels overlap the edges of the shade so there is no gap for light to enter, and a bottom channel or seal closes the sill line. Hunter Douglas builds this into Duette honeycomb shades as [LightLock](https://www.hunterdouglas.com/stories/press-releases/press-release-duette-lightlock): U-shaped side channels lined with micro-ridges that absorb and deflect light before it reaches the room, plus a bottom channel that closes magnetically. Framed systems such as U-Block take the same approach with a powder-coated aluminum frame around the shade. The mechanism is identical in spirit: stop treating the edges as unavoidable.

**Level three: layered blackout.** A blackout shade plus blackout-lined drapery panels over the top. The panels catch whatever escapes the shade, cover the headrail completely, and add insulation and softness a shade alone cannot. Our guide to [using drapes in a room](/blog/how-drapes-make-ceilings-taller-in-coeur-dalene-homes) covers how panels are sized and hung; hung wide and high, they are a light seal as much as a design choice.

## Who needs which level

**Shift workers and day sleepers** are the clearest case for level two. Sleeping against the clock means sleeping through the strongest light of the day, and a soft edge glow at noon is a flood. If you work nights, seal the window.

**Nurseries and kids' rooms** belong at level two or three. Nap schedules run on darkness cues, and a room that goes properly dark at 1 PM is a room a toddler can actually nap in. Specify cordless or motorized operation in these rooms; cords and kids do not mix.

**Light-sensitive sleepers** know who they are. If a hallway light under the door wakes you, a shade halo will too. Level two.

**Media rooms** want level two for a different reason: glare. A sealed blackout system is what makes a projector or a large screen watchable on a bright afternoon.

**Everyone else** can usually start at level one and stop there. And if you already own blackout shades that leak, ask about adding channels before you assume the shades themselves have to be replaced. In many cases the fix is hardware, not new fabric.

## Motorization: the room runs itself

Darkness you have to remember to create is darkness that gets skipped on tired nights. Motorized blackout shades on a schedule close at dusk and open when your day starts, whether you thought about it or not. PowerView automation does this with scenes and schedules, and a Duette with LightLock can run on PowerView, so the sealed system and the schedule come in the same shade.
